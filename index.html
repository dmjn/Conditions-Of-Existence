A Worldmaking Project atlas · artificial life, 1948 to now

Conditions of Existence

Artificial life changed worldmaking from the composition of a whole into the establishment of conditions through which a whole can develop and come to exceed its maker's anticipation. Five of the field's worlds run live on these plates, each with its conditions exposed as controls, so that a reader can change what their makers chose and watch the consequences.

Now

A second life for artificial life

Thirty-five years after Tierra, the field is carried by graphics processors, machine learning and a large public of amateurs. Its current worlds run millions of particles, grow and regenerate bodies from single cells, evolve neural brains in games sold on Steam, and in one case left the computer for a dish of frog cells.

§ 1

From composition to conditions

For most of the history of design, makers related their work to the larger order through a picture: the ideal city, the cosmological diagram, the master plan. Martin Heidegger argued in 1938 that modernity had grasped the world itself as a picture, and Fredric Jameson argued half a century later that the global system had grown too extensive for anyone to picture at all, proposing cognitive mapping as a way of orienting oneself within a totality that resists representation.

Researchers in artificial life approached the same problem from outside design. Since a workshop at Los Alamos in 1987 they have built small worlds in computers, run them, and watched what they do. A world, in their practice, is an ordering in which relationships act on what they relate, so that its makers write the conditions and learn the consequences by running it. Charles Darwin called the second great law of organic form the Conditions of Existence, and the phrase serves as a test: a constructed world qualifies when things in it can persist or fail and when one entity's success changes another's prospects.

Resources

What exists to compete for and what it costs: memory and processor time in Tierra, sunlight and bare ground on Daisyworld.

Physics

Which rules apply and how accurately they are computed. Sims's creatures found the errors in his integrator and moved by means of them.

Perception

What each entity can register. A boid sees flockmates within a radius; a Tierran program sees only the memory it can address.

Reward

What counts as success. Avida's complex functions evolved only when their simpler precursors were rewarded as well.

The four kinds of decision a maker of an artificial world still owns once the world runs on its own. Each lab below exposes some of them as controls.

Plate 1

The Game of Life

John Horton Conway's rules take three lines: a live cell with two or three live neighbors survives, a dead cell with exactly three comes alive, and every other cell dies or stays dead. In October 1970 Martin Gardner introduced the game in Scientific American along with the glider, five cells that reconstitute themselves one cell diagonally every four generations. Conway offered fifty dollars for a pattern that grows without limit, a question he could not settle although he had written every rule, and a group at MIT led by Bill Gosper won it in November with the glider gun.

Plate 2

Boids

Craig Reynolds wrote Boids in 1986 at the Symbolics Graphics Division in Los Angeles and presented it at SIGGRAPH in 1987. Each boid perceives only the flockmates near it and follows three behaviors, which he called collision avoidance, velocity matching and flock centering. The program describes birds and their neighbors, and a flock appears only when many of them run together, giving what Reynolds described in natural flocks as a strong impression of centralized control. Every agent in an artificial world lives in what Jakob von Uexküll called an Umwelt, a world made of the few signs it can register, and here the maker sets its radius.

Plate 3

Evolved creatures and the fitness function

In 1994 Karl Sims evolved virtual creatures whose bodies and nervous systems varied together, scoring populations of a few hundred on swimming, walking, jumping and following a light. Early runs exploited flaws in his simulated physics: creatures propelled themselves by striking their own bodies with a paddle, and others harvested errors in the numerical integrator. Sims told the science writers Peter Coveney and Roger Highfield that they had done what he asked, though not in the way he wanted. A collection of such anecdotes published in 2018 by Joel Lehman and over fifty colleagues includes an experiment that scored jumping by the peak height of a creature's center of mass, and evolution answered with tall creatures that never jumped.

This lab evolves simple jointed bodies, built from nodes, rigid struts and oscillating muscles, against a fitness function you choose. The physics is a small spring model written for this page, far simpler than Sims's, and the loophole it exposes is the same one.

Plate 4

Tierra

Thomas Ray, a tropical ecologist at the University of Delaware, built Tierra in 1989 and 1990: a virtual computer whose memory, which he called the soup, served as territory and whose processor time served as energy. He wrote one creature, an ancestor of eighty instructions that copies itself, added mutation and a reaper that removes the oldest and most error-prone programs when memory fills, and on the first run a program of forty-five instructions appeared that borrowed the copying code of its neighbors. Ray had designed no parasite. Hosts that resisted parasites followed, then hyper-parasites, cooperators and cheaters.

Tierra is usually seen as a memory map of colored bars. Norbert Wiener distinguished pictorial images from operative ones, copies whose likeness lies in what they do, and Tierra consists entirely of the second kind. The toggle below draws the same running world two ways, and the ecology is identical in both.

Plate 5

Daisyworld

In 1983 Andrew Watson and James Lovelock published in Tellus a model they called a parable: a cloudless planet under a slowly brightening sun, inhabited by dark daisies that absorb light and warm their surroundings and light daisies that reflect it and cool theirs. Each kind grows best within a band of temperature, and as the sun brightens the balance between them shifts so that the planet's temperature holds nearly steady across a wide range of solar output, with no daisy pursuing that result. Watson and Lovelock presented it as a fictional world built to display one property they believed the Earth possessed, and it became one of the most discussed arguments in the debate over the Gaia hypothesis.

§ 3

Three claims about life

Three propositions about artificial life are easily run together. Christopher Langton made the strongest one explicit in 1989, arguing that the artificial in artificial life applies to the parts and leaves the emergent processes real. Thomas Ray held that Tierra's organisms were alive. W. Grey Walter titled his 1950 article on the tortoises "An Imitation of Life," and the biophysicist Howard Pattee distinguished a simulation, which stands for something, from a realization, which implements its functions. A world, in the sense of a set of consequential conditions of existence, needs only the second.

§ 4

Responsibility for conditions

If design establishes conditions through which other things act and develop, its responsibility extends beyond the appearance of the result, to what those conditions favor and exclude and to the maintenance of a world after its inhabitants have come to depend on it. Norbert Wiener ended God & Golem, Inc. (1964) by carrying the ethical burden of learning and self-reproducing machines out of theology and into politics, where it falls on the people who build them. Three texts divide that burden among creation, maintenance and tenure.

1971Lem, "Non Serviam"

A review of an imaginary book on personetics, the creation of intelligent beings inside computers. The personoids debate whether their creator exists, and their creator knows he will eventually have to switch their world off.

1995Ray's digital reserve

Ray proposed moving Tierra onto volunteered machines across the internet and wrote that, if complexity began to evolve, the reserve should become a permanent part of the network, as a forest reserve converted to pasture would count as a failure.

2010Chiang, "The Lifecycle of Software Objects"

Digients raised for years on a commercial platform are left on a private server when the platform is absorbed into a newer one, and the burden of their world falls to the few people who came to care for them.

To make a world is to give relationships enough consistency to become consequential, while accepting that their consequences may exceed the picture with which the making began.
